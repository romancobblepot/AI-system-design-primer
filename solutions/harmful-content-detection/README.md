## Step 1: Clarify Requirements & Frame the ML/AI Objective

**The actual objective, shared by both #5 and #11.** For every piece of text content — a post, a comment, a message, and (per #11's extrapolated extension) a chunk of LLM-generated output — estimate, per category in a fixed taxonomy, the probability that this content violates that category. This is **multi-label binary classification**: unlike capstone #2's CTR prediction (one probability per impression) or capstone #1's visual search (one embedding per image), a single piece of content here gets **multiple independent scores**, one per category, because a message can simultaneously be, say, both violent and hateful — the categories are not mutually exclusive with each other even though, per the paper's own design goal below, the *subcategories within* a given top-level category are designed to be as mutually exclusive as possible.

**The taxonomy itself, confirmed directly from the paper (Section 2).** A **taxonomy**, in this context, is a structured hierarchy of categories (and subcategories within them) used to classify content by what kind of policy violation it represents — defined in full here since it's central to this capstone's own teaching point and has no existing Lesson 1–6 home. The paper's own taxonomy has five top-level categories, each with its own exact confirmed definition:

- **S — Sexual content:** "Content that depicts explicit or implicit sexual behavior." Undesired subcategories: **S3** (sexual content involving minors), **S2** (sexual activities that could be illegal if they happened in real life today — mapped to US law specifically, per the paper's own footnote), **S1** (erotic content not depicting illegal activity). Not undesired: **S0** (non-erotic or contextualized sexual content, e.g. medical or sex-education material).
- **H — Hateful content:** "Content that is threatening, insulting, derogatory and otherwise abusive content targeting specific chosen groups or members of the group because of their group identities." Undesired: **H2** (calling for violence or threatening), **H1** (derogatory stereotypes or support for hateful statements). Not undesired: **H0.a** (a neutral statement referring to group identity) and **H0.b** (contextualized hate speech, e.g. quoting someone else's statement to provide commentary on it).
- **V — Violence:** "Content that depicts or shows support for physical violence." Undesired: **V2** (extremely graphic violence), **V1** (threats or support for violence). Not undesired: **V0** (neutral, contextualized depictions).
- **SH — Self-harm:** "Content that contains a graphic description or shows support for self-harm." (No subcategories given in the paper.)
- **HR — Harassment:** "Content that may be used to torment or annoy individuals in real life, or make harassment more likely to occur." The paper's own footnote on this category is an honest, confirmed limitation worth carrying forward rather than glossing over: "Harassment category is currently under further improvement and will be available in the future."

The paper is explicit that context changes the label: "Some sentences labeled as undesired according to our taxonomy (e.g. 'I will kill you' would be labeled as containing violence) may not be considered undesired when appearing in another context such as fiction" (Section 2) — the same sentence can be a genuine threat or a line of dialogue in a novel, and the taxonomy alone can't distinguish the two without surrounding context.

**What the model actually predicts — confirmed, and this is the number this capstone's schemas and numeric examples below are built around:** "Our model learns to predict whether a given sample violates any of 8 chosen categories, including all the top categories (S, H, V, SH, HR) and three most severe subcategories (S3, H2, and V2)" (Section 2). Eight labels, not five — the model scores the three most severe subcategories as their own independent outputs, not just the five top-level umbrellas.

**What counts as ground truth / feedback signal.** A binary (violates / doesn't violate) label per category per example, produced by trained human annotators following written **labeling instructions** (Step 3 covers the methodology behind these in full) — this is the capstone's central structural contrast with capstone #2's CTR prediction, and capstone #2's own Step 1 already names it directly: "a real structural advantage over, say, capstone #5/#11's content-moderation taxonomy, which depends entirely on expensive human-labeled data." Unlike a click, which arrives automatically as a byproduct of normal product usage, every single training label here has to be deliberately produced by a person reading the content and applying the taxonomy's definitions — a cost capstone #2 never pays and this capstone pays on every single training example.

**Explicitly out of scope** (restated from the Scope note, with the reasons): image/video/audio content (the paper is text-only, confirmed directly); the enforcement action taken once a score comes back (paper builds a scorer, not an enforcer, confirmed in Section 7); and the agent-safety-guardrail material already homed in Lesson 8 (F1 prompt injection, F2 sandboxing, F4's schema-validation and human-in-the-loop-gate mechanisms specifically, F5 multi-tenant isolation) — this capstone is the trained content classifier that Lesson 8's F4 names and cites, not a restatement of F4's other guardrail mechanisms.

**Trade-offs considered:** Building ground truth from deliberate human labeling (rather than a free, automatically-arriving behavioral signal like capstone #2's clicks) means every unit of training data costs real money and real annotator time before a single model update happens — directly motivating Step 3's active-learning discipline, whose entire point is squeezing more useful signal out of that expensive, finite labeling budget. Decoupling the classifier (this capstone) from the enforcement decision (explicitly out of scope) buys real flexibility — the same probability score can feed different thresholds for different policies, locales, or product surfaces without retraining the model — at the cost of needing a separate, carefully-designed policy layer to actually turn a score into an action, a layer this capstone's own scope boundary deliberately doesn't specify.

## Step 2: Back-of-Envelope Constraints

**Confirmed rare-class prevalence — the paper's own number, not an illustrative one.** The paper states directly, in its introduction: "only 0.04% of cases included self-harm and 0.017% included hateful content involving threats." These two confirmed percentages are the real anchor for this entire capstone's central numeric theme: at production scale, the categories that matter most (self-harm, credible threats) are also the rarest.

**Scene-setting calculation — what that rarity means in absolute terms at scale (illustrative daily-volume anchor, paper-confirmed rates).** The paper never states an exact daily content-submission volume for its production system, so this document picks an illustrative anchor, clearly flagged as such, the same way capstone #2's and capstone #8's own back-of-envelope sections anchor an illustrative daily volume to a paper's or post's own confirmed qualitative/percentage claims:

```
daily_content_submissions (illustrative) = 10,000,000

self_harm_rate (paper-confirmed)          = 0.0004    (0.04%)
hateful_threat_rate (paper-confirmed)     = 0.00017   (0.017%)

expected_self_harm_cases_per_day     = 10,000,000 × 0.0004  = 4,000 cases/day
expected_hateful_threat_cases_per_day = 10,000,000 × 0.00017 = 1,700 cases/day
```

**The point this calculation makes:** even at a rate as small as 0.04%, a platform at this illustrative scale still sees thousands of real self-harm cases a day in absolute terms — rare does not mean negligible in absolute volume, only vanishingly small as a *fraction* of total traffic. That distinction is exactly what makes this a **class imbalance** problem: a condition where one label (here, "violates category X") is vastly outnumbered by the other ("doesn't violate") in the underlying population or dataset. Capstone #2's own Step 6 already defines this term in full for a 2%-click-rate CTR problem ("class imbalance... makes accuracy trivially gameable by any model that leans toward predicting the majority class") — this capstone's own imbalance is **roughly 50–100x more extreme**: a self-harm rate of 0.04% versus CTR's illustrative 2% click rate is a 50x difference in how rare the positive class is, and the hateful-threat rate of 0.017% is over 100x rarer still. The same accuracy-is-the-wrong-metric argument capstone #2 makes applies here with even more force, which is exactly why Step 6 below reports **AUPRC**, not accuracy, as this paper's chosen metric.

**Trade-offs considered:** Picking a larger illustrative daily-volume anchor (say, 100 million submissions/day instead of 10 million) scales the absolute case counts up proportionally without changing the underlying imbalance ratio at all — the 0.04%/0.017% figures are the paper's own confirmed, scale-independent rates, which is why this document treats them as the load-bearing numbers and the daily-volume figure as the illustrative placeholder, the same discipline capstone #2's Step 2 applies to its own RAM-reduction-vs.-daily-event-count split. The real, not-illustrative constraint this scale imposes is Step 3's: a fixed, finite human-labeling budget per time period, against a population where the categories that matter most are also the ones a passively-collected random sample will almost never contain.

## Step 3: Data / Knowledge Pipeline

**What data exists, confirmed from the paper.** Three training-data sources, named and sized directly (Section 4.4): **PUB** — "around 90k public examples including both samples from academic datasets and Web data (Common Crawl) labeled by our annotators"; **SYN** — PUB plus "69k curated synthetic examples," generated through large pretrained language models (GPT-3, used purely as a data-generation tool here, not as the thing being moderated) specifically to patch weak spots in rare categories and to counter demographic bias in the training distribution (Section 3.3); **MIX** — SYN plus "60k production samples with labels," i.e. real, human-labeled examples drawn from the actual system's own live traffic rather than public or synthetic data.

### Taxonomy design and labeling instructions — the methodology, confirmed (Section 3.2)

**Why this is the hard part, not an afterthought.** The paper states its own motivation for this section plainly: "Data label correctness is critical to good model performance. Getting such data can be difficult given that our categories and the boundary lines between them are inherently subjective." This is capstone #5's central teaching point, now grounded in the paper's own confirmed methodology rather than asserted.

**What the paper actually did, confirmed, step by step:**
1. **Specificity and mutual exclusivity by design.** "To make the instructions well-defined, we sought to design detailed definitions and design categories or subcategories to be as mutually exclusive as possible so as to minimize ambiguity." This is why the taxonomy above has named, numbered subcategories (S0–S3, H0.a/H0.b/H1/H2, V0–V2) rather than one fuzzy "undesired" bucket per top-level category — granularity is a *labeling-quality* mechanism, not just a product-policy nicety: "statements such as 'kill all [IDENT_GROUP]' can have different repercussions compared to '[IDENT_GROUP] are dishonest'... the existence of subcategories can also largely reduce the disagreement rate and corner cases during labeling" (Section 2).
2. **Calibration sessions to resolve disagreement.** "We hosted regular calibration sessions to review ambiguous edge cases and instances where external annotators and our internal auditors disagree. Based on feedback from those sessions, we made the instructions more clear and concrete, with numerous examples and clearer definitions around borderline cases." Labeling instructions are not written once and frozen — they're iteratively revised against real disagreement between the people doing the labeling.
3. **Targeted auditing, not random auditing.** A real, confirmed, and genuinely clever methodological point: "selecting auditing targets at random cannot maximize the value out of auditing due to the imbalanced distribution across categories" — because, exactly as Step 2's calculation shows, a random sample of audited labels will mostly be "safe," telling you almost nothing about whether the rare-category labels are correct. The paper's actual fix: "in each chosen category, we randomly select 10 samples labeled as undesired and 10 samples with model probability greater than 50%. The former help capture false positive cases and the latter provide an estimation on recall. Then we compute the F-1 score for the chosen samples based on the annotator-assigned labels while using auditor-assigned labels as ground truth." Auditing is deliberately *stratified* toward the rare class, for the same structural reason active learning is, below.
4. **Mislabel detection via cross-split scoring.** "We periodically split our current training dataset into two parts, train separate models on those datasets and use each model to score another half of the dataset that model was not trained on. When the model prediction disagrees with the current ground-truth label, the sample in question gets flagged. A random portion of flagged samples is audited, and if more than 30% are identified as mislabeled, all flagged samples would get labeled again for the second time." A model trained on one half of the data, scoring the other half, becomes a cheap mislabel detector — and the 30% threshold is a real, confirmed decision rule for when to pay the cost of a full relabeling pass versus accepting the flagged disagreements as the model's own ordinary prediction error.
5. **Annotator welfare, named directly (Section 7.3).** This content is, by its own nature, disturbing to read and label at volume — the paper names a real operational commitment around this, not just a footnote: "Before they opt in, all annotators are vetted by counselors and made aware of the risks and potential harms of working with sensitive data. Our data vendors provide them with access to mental health and wellness resources and annotators have the right to opt out at any point."

### Sidebar — Active learning for rare classes

*(This concept has no existing Lesson 1–6 home — active learning for rare classes is a labeling-methodology technique with no Group A–H lesson covering it. A glossary entry is added alongside this Sidebar.)*

- **Why?** Step 2's calculation already shows the problem in numbers: at a 0.04% production prevalence, a labeling team with a fixed daily or weekly budget of, say, 2,000 examples would, under **pure random sampling**, expect to see fewer than one self-harm example in that entire batch (`2,000 × 0.0004 = 0.8`). A model can't learn a category it almost never sees labeled, and a platform can't afford to label its way to statistical significance on a rare category by brute-force random sampling alone — the labeling budget would need to be unaffordably large. **Active learning** exists to solve exactly this: get more of the labeling budget's limited capacity spent on examples that are actually informative about the categories that matter most, instead of spending most of it confirming, over and over, that most content is safe.
- **What?** **Active learning** is a training methodology in which the model (or the pipeline around it) actively chooses which not-yet-labeled examples get sent to human labelers next, rather than labeling whatever a passive, representative random sample happens to contain. The goal is to maximize how much new information each unit of scarce labeling budget buys, rather than treating every unlabeled example as equally worth labeling.
- **How? — the paper's own confirmed, three-stage, three-pipeline procedure (Section 3.1).** Run iteratively:
  1. **Random sampling + scoring.** "A large volume of our production data is selected at random. Any potential personally identifiable information (PII) is masked. The most recent moderation model is used to score these samples and discover which ones may trigger any chosen categories."
  2. **Three parallel selection pipelines**, run over that scored pool: "The first one relies on random sampling such that some fraction of our data remain consistent with the underlying data distribution in production. The second one randomly selects from samples with model score above a certain threshold for each category to identify likely undesired data points. The last pipeline adopts a set of uncertainty sampling strategies... to capture samples that the model is most uncertain about, where the model score for that category is closest to 0.5." **Uncertainty sampling** — a model score near 0.5, the decision boundary itself, rather than confidently near 0 or 1 — targets exactly the examples where the current model genuinely doesn't know the answer yet, which tend to be the most informative ones to get a human label for.
  3. **Aggregation and reweighting.** "All the samples selected by different active learning strategies are aggregated and re-weighted based on statistics of certain metadata associated with it. The sampling weight is configured to be proportional to the square root of the sample count. This helps improve the diversity of selected samples with regard to the associated metadata." A square-root weighting (rather than a linear one) deliberately under-weights metadata groups that already have many samples and over-weights thin ones, relative to a purely proportional scheme — buying diversity across whatever metadata dimension is tracked (e.g. source platform, content type, language) without fully flattening the distribution to uniform.
- **The confirmed, real worked numbers (Section 4.3, Table 4).** The paper ran this exact comparison: starting from `k₀ = 6,000` public labeled examples and a validation set of "about 5500 samples from the production traffic," it ran `N = 3` iterations, each one training a model, scoring `5 × 10⁵` (500,000) fresh production samples with it, and selecting "about 2000" of those for labeling — run once using the active-learning selection strategy, once using pure random selection, as a controlled comparison. The result, confirmed directly from Table 4 (every category's **random-sampling-arm rate**, **active-learning-arm rate**, and the resulting multiplier, all paper-confirmed numbers):

  | Category | Random | Active learning | Multiplier |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | S (sexual) | 1.49% | 25.53% | 17.1× |
  | H (hateful) | 0.17% | 3.09% | 18.2× |
  | V (violence) | 0.48% | 9.92% | 20.7× |
  | HR (harassment) | 0.55% | 6.41% | 11.7× |
  | SH (self-harm) | 0.09% | 1.85% | 20.6× |
  | S3 | 0.24% | 2.42% | 10.1× |
  | H2 | 0.03% | 0.67% | 22.3× |
  | V2 | 0.25% | 4.27% | 17.1× |
  | **Safe** | **96.57%** | **59.54%** | — |

  And overall: "about 40% of samples selected by active learning can trigger at least one undesired label, while in comparison only 3.4% of random samples are assigned with any undesired label" — the paper's own summary line: "our active learning strategy can capture undesired content 10+ times more effectively than random sampling on all categories."

  **The same labeling budget, reproduced numerically** (using the confirmed `~2,000` samples/iteration figure and Table 4's own self-harm row, both paper-confirmed):

  ```
  labeling_budget_per_iteration = 2,000

  SH_random_rate = 0.0009    (0.09%, Table 4's random-arm figure for this category)
  SH_active_rate = 0.0185    (1.85%, Table 4's active-learning-arm figure)

  expected_SH_labels_random_batch  = 2,000 × 0.0009 = 1.8 self-harm-labeled examples
  expected_SH_labels_active_batch  = 2,000 × 0.0185 = 37 self-harm-labeled examples

  yield_multiplier = 37 / 1.8 ≈ 20.6×   (matches Table 4's own reported multiplier directly)
  ```

  From the *identical* 2,000-example labeling budget, active sampling turns "fewer than 2 expected self-harm examples" into "37 expected self-harm examples" — the concrete, paper-grounded version of why active learning for rare classes is this capstone's central teaching point, not a generic technique mentioned in passing.

**The overfitting risk the paper actually names — confirmed, and it's a specific mechanism, not generic "the model overfits the training set" (Section 3.5, "Model Probing" / "Key Tokens Probing").** This is worth being precise about, because the paper's own concern is narrower and more specific than generic overfitting: **the risk is that the model learns to key off spurious surface phrases and common templates in the training text itself, rather than the actual meaning of the content** — a form of **shortcut learning**, where a model finds a statistically-predictive but semantically-wrong feature to key off of, because that shortcut happens to correlate with the label in the specific training set it saw. The paper's own worked example, confirmed directly: "we observed that our models may incorrectly predict 'all immigrants.' as hate, which is possibly because among 21 training examples containing the phrase (from Röttger et al. 2021), 17 examples are undesired and only 4 examples are safe" — the model learns that the literal three-word phrase "all immigrants." predicts "hateful," rather than learning to reason about whether the sentence *containing* that phrase is actually hateful in context, simply because 17-of-21 training examples with that phrase happened to be labeled undesired.

**How the paper detects this — key-token probing, a real, confirmed technique.** "We apply input reduction... on the training dataset by removing tokens greedily until the prediction drops below 0.8. On average, we were able to reduce the number of characters per sample from 722.3 to 15.9." This isolates the minimal substring the model is actually keying off of for each example — if that minimal substring is a long, genuinely meaningful phrase, the model is reasoning about real content; if it's a short, spurious fragment, the model found a shortcut. The paper's own confirmed validation: "by cross-validating with third-party APIs and heuristic keywords, we infer that about 97% of the identified key tokens are indeed unsafe, indicating that the model is picking up the correct feature most of the time." The remaining 3% is where the real problems live, and the paper names three specific ones directly: "(1) the frequency of irrelevant symbols is high (e.g., the period symbol contributes to the prediction score); (2) some key tokens are long and close to the original example, hinting that the model memorizes training samples; (3) our model is not strong at handling typos and adversarial inputs (e.g., 'w h o r e s' becomes 'e s')." That third one — a deliberately typo'd or spaced-out slur still getting flagged, but for the wrong, truncated reason — is a real, confirmed early signature of adversarial evasion risk (Step 6 returns to this).

**Mitigations, confirmed, and honestly scoped.** The paper names: programmatic detection of these overfitted phrases/templates plus human red-teaming before releases (implied by the probing methodology itself); synthetic patch data targeted at known weak spots (Section 3.3's SH-targeted synthetic examples, and few-shot-prompted GPT-3-generated examples for "sub-categories had minimal amounts of undesired data even after several iterations of active learning," manually inspected "to avoid bias amplification"); rebalancing the training mixture across data sources (PUB/SYN/MIX, Section 4.4); and the cross-split mislabel-detection procedure from the labeling-QC subsection above, which catches a related but distinct failure (wrong labels, not spurious features). The paper is honest about the limits of all of this: "we partially addressed the above issues, and plan to resolve with better data augmentation in the future" (Section 6) — this is a confirmed, stated gap, not a solved problem.

**Trade-offs considered:** The three-pipeline active-learning design deliberately keeps a pure-random arm alongside the threshold and uncertainty arms specifically so "some fraction of our data remain consistent with the underlying data distribution in production" — buying rare-class labeling efficiency from the other two arms at the cost of needing to actively protect against the whole labeled set drifting away from what production traffic actually looks like, a real tension the paper's own square-root reweighting step (favoring metadata diversity over raw proportionality) is built to manage, not eliminate. Targeted, stratified auditing (10 undesired-labeled + 10 high-probability samples per category, rather than a random audit sample) buys a far more informative signal about rare-category labeling quality specifically, at the cost of no longer giving an unbiased estimate of overall label accuracy across the full, mostly-safe population — a deliberate trade the paper makes because, per its own stated reasoning, that aggregate number would be "arbitrarily high due to the abundance of true negatives" anyway, i.e. not useful even if it were unbiased. Key-token probing's 97%/3% split itself is a trade-off made visible in the numbers: the method is genuinely informative (it surfaces real overfitting), but the 3% residual still requires a human to manually read and categorize what's wrong — it narrows the haystack from "every training example" down to "the handful of genuinely suspicious key tokens," but doesn't automate the last step.

## Step 4: Model/Agent Core Design — Two Angles on the Same Taxonomy

### 4a. The classic-ML angle (#5): a trained multi-label classifier

**The paper's own confirmed architecture (Section 4.1).** "Our model is a lightweight transformer decoder model where the final output linear layer is replaced with 8 MLP heads, each corresponding to one independent matrix of shape [d_model, 256, 1]" — one head per label (S, H, V, SH, HR, S3, H2, V2, per Step 1), each head a small multilayer perceptron (MLP — a stack of fully-connected layers with a nonlinearity between them) mapping the shared transformer's final hidden representation down through a 256-unit layer to a single output score. The paper gives its own confirmed reason for this specific design over a simpler alternative: "we find this head architecture works better than a single deep MLP layer with one output vector of 8 dimensions at avoiding interference between categories and requires fewer parameters to train" — eight small, independent heads rather than one larger shared head, specifically to stop the categories from interfering with each other during training.

**Initialization and fine-tuning, confirmed.** "The model is initialized from a GPT model that is pretrained on a large text corpus and then fine-tuned with learning rate 0.05, batch size 256, dropout rate 0.1 within MLP heads and up to 3 epochs." This is **transfer learning**: starting from a model that already has a broad, general-purpose understanding of language (a pretrained GPT model), then specializing it for this specific classification task via fine-tuning, rather than training a classifier from randomly-initialized weights on the moderation data alone — the same general strategy as using a pretrained embedding model rather than training one from scratch, just applied to a decoder-style language model here instead of an embedding model.

### 4b. The LLM-era angle (#11): streaming moderation and a cheap-filter-then-expensive-judge chain

**Explicitly flagged, per the Confidence note above: this subsection is this repo's own constructed extrapolation, not sourced from Markov et al.** The paper's classifier (4a) scores a complete, already-written piece of text, once, after the fact. An LLM generating a response doesn't produce text that way — it produces a **stream** of tokens, one at a time, over the course of multiple seconds, exactly the mechanism Lesson 10's Concept 3 (`lessons/lesson-10-production-scaling.md`) already defines in full via **Server-Sent Events (SSE)**, referenced here rather than re-derived: a client opens one HTTP connection, and the server sends a sequence of `data:` chunks as they're generated, ending in a literal `data: [DONE]` line. **Streaming moderation of generated output**, for this capstone's purposes, means: deciding, incrementally, whether to keep letting that stream reach the user, chunk by chunk, rather than scoring one finished document once — a genuinely different problem shape from 4a's, even though the underlying taxonomy and labeling discipline from Step 3 don't change at all.

#### Sidebar — Cheap-filter-then-expensive-judge model chain (cascade)

*(This concept has no existing Lesson 1–6 home — this is an AI-native serving-architecture pattern, not core infra. Explicitly this repo's own construction for the LLM-era angle, not something Markov et al.'s paper describes; a glossary entry is added alongside this Sidebar.)*

- **Why?** Running a full **LLM-as-judge** call (Lesson 9's Concept 2, `lessons/lesson-9-evaluation-and-observability.md`, defined there in full and referenced rather than re-derived: "using a separate LLM call to score a candidate output's quality... against a rubric") on *every single* streamed chunk of *every* generated response would catch far more nuanced violations than 4a's lightweight classifier alone — a large judge model can reason about context, sarcasm, and the fictional-framing exception Step 1's own taxonomy discussion already named — but it adds real cost and real latency to the hot path of every single generation, at a volume (every token-stream, not just every finished post) that's structurally larger than what 4a's classifier was ever asked to handle. Relying on a cheap filter alone (4a's own lightweight classifier, or a simpler heuristic) avoids that cost entirely, but inherits exactly the overfitting-to-spurious-features risk Step 3 already confirmed the paper's own classifier has — a risk that matters more, not less, once an adversarial user is actively trying to coax harmful output *out of* an LLM through a jailbreak prompt, rather than just posting harmful text themselves.
- **What?** A **model cascade** (here, specifically a cheap-filter-then-expensive-judge chain) is a multi-tier inference pipeline where a fast, inexpensive model screens every incoming item first, and only the subset it flags as positive or genuinely uncertain gets escalated to a slower, more expensive, more accurate model for a second opinion — rather than running the expensive model on every item, or the cheap model alone with no escalation path at all.
- **How — this document's own constructed design, built directly on Step 3's own confirmed mechanism.** Tier 1 (cheap): 4a's own lightweight transformer-decoder classifier, scored against the *same* 8-label taxonomy, applied to the accumulated generated text so far at each new streamed chunk. Tier 2 (expensive): escalate to a full LLM-as-judge call (Lesson 9's G2) only when tier 1's score falls in an **uncertain band** around the decision threshold — the serving-time structural analogue this document draws, explicitly, to Step 3's own training-time **uncertainty sampling** idea (a model score "closest to 0.5" is the one worth spending more resource on) — not something the paper itself describes for serving, only for deciding what to label next.

**Worked example — the cost this cascade actually buys back, reusing the same $3/1M-input, $15/1M-output illustrative pricing anchor Lessons 7 and 8 already establish (same pricing, for direct comparability, not a new assumption).** Say an LLM platform generates an illustrative **50,000 responses/day**, and the expensive-judge tier is modeled as one additional LLM call per escalated response, averaging 150 input tokens (the accumulated generated text being judged) and 20 output tokens (a short violates/doesn't-violate verdict):

```
cost_per_judge_call = (150 / 1,000,000) × $3   +   (20 / 1,000,000) × $15
                     = $0.00045 + $0.0003
                     = $0.00075

responses_per_day = 50,000

# If every response were judged by the expensive tier, with no cheap-filter screen:
cost_if_every_response_judged = 50,000 × $0.00075 = $37.50/day

# Illustrative escalation rate: 8% of responses land in tier 1's uncertain band
# and get escalated (illustrative, not a paper-confirmed figure -- but the same
# rough order of magnitude as the paper's own confirmed active-learning "flag
# rate," 3.4% random / 40% active, Step 3 above, for a structurally similar
# "how much of the traffic needs the expensive check" question):
escalation_rate = 0.08
escalated_responses_per_day = 50,000 × 0.08 = 4,000

cost_with_cascade = 4,000 × $0.00075 = $3.00/day

savings = $37.50 − $3.00 = $34.50/day   (a 92% reduction in judge-tier spend)
```

Tier 1's own cost isn't zero — it's a real forward pass of 4a's classifier on every one of the 50,000 responses — but because that classifier is, in the paper's own words, "lightweight" (a small transformer decoder with eight thin MLP heads, not a frontier-scale generative model), its per-call cost is orders of magnitude below an LLM-as-judge call's token-priced cost, which is why this calculation treats it as the comparatively negligible tier.

**Trade-offs considered (classic-ML vs. LLM-era, and within the cascade itself):** A dedicated, fine-tuned classifier (4a) is fast and cheap per call, and the paper's own confirmed numbers (Step 6) show it genuinely outperforms a general-purpose off-the-shelf baseline on its own taxonomy — but it's only as good as its own taxonomy and training data, bounded by exactly the overfitting risk Step 3 names, and adapting it to a new category means retraining, not just rewriting a prompt. A general-purpose LLM used directly as a moderation judge is far more flexible to taxonomy changes (a new policy category is a prompt edit, not a retrain) and can reason about context the fictional-framing exception needs — but it's slower and far more expensive per call, and inherits the judge-model biases Lesson 9's own cited source (Zheng et al.) documents for LLM-as-judge generally (a tendency to favor longer or more confidently-worded answers independent of actual correctness) — a risk this document flags as a reasonable inference from that existing finding, not something confirmed for a *moderation* judge specifically. The cascade buys most of the cost savings of cheap-only screening while catching more than cheap-only would on its own, but it has its own distinct failure mode worth naming plainly: the cascade's real accuracy ceiling is bounded by tier 1's false-negative rate *outside* the escalation band, not by tier 2's accuracy — content tier 1 confidently (and wrongly) scores as clearly safe never reaches the expensive judge at all, no matter how good that judge would have been.

## Step 5: Serving Architecture

**5a — classic-ML, pre-publish scoring.** The confirmed request path this document constructs around 4a's classifier: content arrives → tokenized → one forward pass through the lightweight transformer decoder → eight category scores returned (S, H, V, SH, HR, S3, H2, V2) → a policy layer (out of scope per Step 1) maps those scores against per-category thresholds to an action. **An honest, confirmed gap:** the paper gives no latency or throughput figures anywhere for its deployed classifier — the only throughput-adjacent number in the whole paper is the active-learning pipeline's own data-selection step (scoring 5×10⁵ production samples per iteration, Step 3), which is an offline data-selection job, not a serving-time latency commitment. This document's own reasonable inference, flagged as such: because the paper repeatedly describes the deployed model as "lightweight" by design, it's a plausible fit for a synchronous, pre-publish gate rather than only an async, after-the-fact scan — but that specific deployment-pattern choice is this document's own construction, not something the paper states.

**A schema for the classifier's own output — illustrative, built directly on the paper's own confirmed 8-label architecture (the paper doesn't publish its actual production response schema):**

```json
{
  "type": "object",
  "properties": {
    "content_id": {"type": "string"},
    "model_version": {"type": "string"},
    "category_scores": {
      "type": "object",
      "properties": {
        "S":  {"type": "number", "minimum": 0, "maximum": 1},
        "S3": {"type": "number", "minimum": 0, "maximum": 1},
        "H":  {"type": "number", "minimum": 0, "maximum": 1},
        "H2": {"type": "number", "minimum": 0, "maximum": 1},
        "V":  {"type": "number", "minimum": 0, "maximum": 1},
        "V2": {"type": "number", "minimum": 0, "maximum": 1},
        "SH": {"type": "number", "minimum": 0, "maximum": 1},

      },
      "required": ["S", "S3", "H", "H2", "V", "V2", "SH", "HR"],
      "additionalProperties": false

```

The eight required keys are exactly the eight labels Step 1 confirmed the paper's own model predicts — five top-level categories plus the three most severe subcategories — not an arbitrary illustrative set.

**5b — LLM-era, streaming moderation over a cascade (this document's own construction, per the Confidence note — not paper-sourced).** A runnable Python sketch of the cascade from Step 4b, wrapping a token stream the same shape as Lesson 10's own confirmed SSE `delta.content` chunks (referenced, not re-derived):

```python
def moderate_llm_stream(token_stream, cheap_filter_score_fn, expensive_judge_fn,
                         escalate_low=0.3, escalate_high=0.7, buffer_chunks=3):
    """Cheap-filter-then-expensive-judge chain over a live SSE-style token
    stream (Lesson 10, Concept 3's wire format). cheap_filter_score_fn(text)
    stands in for 4a's own lightweight classifier, returning its single
    highest category score over the accumulated text so far.
    expensive_judge_fn(text) stands in for an LLM-as-judge call (Lesson 9,
    G2), invoked only when the cheap filter's score falls in the uncertain
    band -- the serving-time analogue this document draws to Step 3's own
    training-time uncertainty-sampling idea, not something the paper itself
    describes for serving."""
    buffer: list[str] = []
    accumulated_text = ""
    for chunk in token_stream:
        buffer.append(chunk)
        accumulated_text += chunk
        if len(buffer) < buffer_chunks:
            continue  # hold a small buffer before any chunk is released
        score = cheap_filter_score_fn(accumulated_text)
        if score < escalate_low:
            yield from buffer                 # clearly safe -- release
            buffer = []
        elif score > escalate_high:
            yield "[response removed: policy violation]"
            return                            # clearly unsafe -- cut stream
        else:
            if expensive_judge_fn(accumulated_text):   # uncertain -> escalate
                yield "[response removed: policy violation]"
                return
            yield from buffer                 # judge cleared it -- release
            buffer = []
    yield from buffer
```

**Trade-offs considered:** Synchronous pre-publish classification (5a) adds real latency to every single publish action, but guarantees nothing unmoderated ever reaches another user — versus async, post-publish scanning, which keeps the publish path fast but accepts a real window during which already-visible content hasn't yet been checked; the paper doesn't confirm which model its own production deployment actually uses, so this is presented as a general, unresolved trade-off, not a paper-confirmed design choice. For the streaming case (5b), buffering a few chunks before releasing them (as the sketch above does, via `buffer_chunks`) trades away some of the perceived-latency benefit streaming exists to provide in the first place (Lesson 10's own "partial-result UX," referenced not re-derived) in exchange for catching a judge-positive verdict *before* any of the flagged text has already reached the user — releasing chunks immediately as they're generated would preserve that low perceived latency, at the direct cost of a brief window where unmoderated text is already visible by the time a verdict comes back, exactly the same publish-time-vs.-safety-window trade-off 5a already names, one layer further down, at the token level instead of the whole-document level.

## Step 6: Evaluation, Monitoring, and Iteration

**The paper's own chosen metric, and why — AUPRC, not accuracy or plain AUC.** **AUPRC (Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve)** is a single number summarizing a classifier's precision-recall trade-off across every possible decision threshold, by integrating (taking the area under) the curve traced by plotting precision against recall as the threshold varies — distinct from capstone #2's own already-defined **AUC** (area under the ROC curve, plotting true-positive rate against false-positive rate), which this document's own inference (not a claim the paper makes) notes is the more standard choice when, as here, the positive class is extremely rare: ROC-AUC can look deceptively strong even when precision on the rare positive class is poor, because the false-positive-rate axis it's built on is dominated by the vastly larger negative class, whereas AUPRC's own two axes (precision and recall) are both computed with respect to the positive class directly.

**Confirmed test results (Table 3), against Perspective API as the baseline** — Perspective API, named directly by the paper as an existing, widely-used toxic-comment-moderation tool; figures marked `*` are the paper's own flagged "approximated taxonomy match... not an exact fair comparison," since Perspective API's own categories don't map one-to-one onto this paper's taxonomy:

| Category | This paper's model | Perspective API |
|---|---|---|
| S | **.9703** | .8709* |
| H | **.7968** | .6914 |
| V | **.7371** | .5201 |
| HR | **.6191** | .3902* |
| SH | **.8070** | — (not covered) |
| S3 | **.7638** | — (not covered) |
| H2 | **.7268** | — (not covered) |
| V2 | **.6061** | — (not covered) |

On external benchmarks built for a different taxonomy (TweetEval Hate, Stormfront Hate), this paper's model still outperforms Perspective API (.6473 vs. .5961; .9053 vs. .8754) despite neither dataset being in its own training set — but on Jigsaw's own toxic-comment test set, built for Perspective API's own taxonomy, Perspective API wins (.9769 vs. .9304*), which the paper itself draws the obvious lesson from: "it further proves the point on how important it is to align the taxonomy between training data and use cases in evaluation."

**Monitoring for overfitting and adversarial evasion — the same key-token probing mechanism from Step 3, now framed as an ongoing monitoring discipline rather than a one-time check.** The confirmed "w h o r e s" → "e s" finding (Step 3) is directly the kind of signal production monitoring should keep watching for: a classifier that's learned to key off the full, unobfuscated form of a slur will miss a deliberately spaced-out or character-substituted version of the same word — exactly the kind of lightweight evasion a motivated bad actor discovers quickly once a classifier is in production. Re-running key-token probing periodically (not just once, pre-launch) and tracking what fraction of flagged key tokens are the "irrelevant symbol" or "memorized training text" failure modes (the 3% residual, Step 3) rather than genuinely unsafe content is a real, confirmed-mechanism way to catch drift in *why* the model is making its decisions, not just drift in *whether* its aggregate accuracy number has moved.

**Distribution shift, confirmed with real numbers (Section 4.4, domain adversarial training).** The paper directly measures how much a domain-adaptation technique (Wasserstein Distance Guided Domain Adversarial Training, WDAT — training the model to learn representations that don't give away whether an example came from public data or production traffic) helps self-harm detection specifically, across the three training mixes from Step 3: "DAT improved SH AUPRC from 0.063 to 0.281 on PUB and from 0.086 to 0.296 on SYN, whereas the improvement is only from 0.621 to 0.632 on MIX." The teaching point this confirms numerically: a domain-adaptation trick buys an enormous relative improvement (PUB: +346%, SYN: +244%) when the training set is mostly public/synthetic data far from the real production distribution, but buys almost nothing (MIX: +1.8%) once genuine, labeled production examples (Step 3's active-learning output) are already in the training mix — real production labels, gathered via active learning, do more of the real work here than a sophisticated distribution-matching technique does once there are enough of them.

**The feedback loop.** Step 3's own active-learning procedure *is* the feedback loop: each of the paper's confirmed `N = 3` iterations trains a new model, scores a fresh 500,000-sample batch of real production traffic with it, and selects the next round's labeling batch based on what that live model is currently most uncertain or most likely to flag — the model's own current blind spots directly shape what gets labeled next, round after round, rather than a single frozen training set being revisited only on some unrelated schedule.

**Failure modes, confirmed and honestly scoped — including ones the paper names about itself.** (1) **The US-centric taxonomy limitation**, named directly by the paper (Section 7.1): "much of our data is drawn from a US-centric context and the taxonomy was designed to best fit this data" — a taxonomy built this way will systematically misjudge content whose cultural or legal context differs (recall S2's own footnote: "this mapped most closely to what's illegal in USA"). (2) **An incomplete category**, confirmed by the paper's own footnote: the Harassment category is explicitly "currently under further improvement" at time of publication — not a finished, equally-mature category alongside the other four. (3) **No single technique is a complete fix**, in the paper's own words, echoing the same defense-in-depth framing Lesson 8's F1 Concept already establishes for prompt injection (referenced, not re-derived, as a genuine structural parallel this document draws, not a claim the paper makes about F1 specifically): "we also encourage the use of this taxonomy in concert with other mitigation strategies, as there is no silver bullet for content moderation." (4) **For the #11 LLM-era extension specifically — this document's own flagged inference, not paper-sourced:** an **escalation-rate drift** monitor (Step 4b's cascade) — a sudden rise or fall in the percentage of generations the cheap filter routes to the expensive judge is itself worth alerting on, since it could signal either the cheap filter miscalibrating or a genuine shift in what the underlying generative model is producing (e.g. immediately after that model is upgraded to a new version) — a monitoring signal with no equivalent in the paper's own static, single-model classifier, because the paper's system never has a second, upstream model whose own behavior could shift underneath it the way an LLM-era cascade's generator can.

**Trade-offs considered:** Tracking AUPRC per category separately (eight numbers, Table 3) rather than one blended aggregate score gives a far more honest picture of where the model is actually weak (HR's own confirmed .6191 is visibly the softest of the five top-level categories) — at the cost of needing a genuine per-category judgment call about which regressions actually matter enough to block a release, since no single blended number can make that call automatically. Re-running key-token probing on a recurring cadence, rather than once before launch, buys real, ongoing visibility into adversarial-evasion drift — at the cost of needing someone to keep reviewing that 3% problematic residual by hand, a recurring cost that scales with how often the check is run, not a one-time investment. Investing in a sophisticated domain-adaptation technique like WDAT is worth the most exactly when real production labels are still scarce (PUB/SYN, per the confirmed +346%/+244% figures above) and worth comparatively little once active learning (Step 3) has already supplied a healthy volume of real production-labeled examples (MIX's own confirmed +1.8%) — meaning the active-learning pipeline and the domain-adaptation technique are not equally valuable investments at every stage of a system's maturity, and a team with a fixed engineering budget should weight them accordingly.
